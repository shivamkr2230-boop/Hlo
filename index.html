Chapter 1: Cell — The Unit of Life (Class 11 NEET Foundation)

Every living thing you can name — a banyan tree, a blue whale, or a single bacterium floating in a pond — is built from the same basic unit: the cell. It is the smallest structure capable of carrying out all the functions of life independently: it can take in nutrients, convert them into energy, grow, respond to its surroundings, and reproduce. This idea took nearly two centuries of careful microscopy and debate to become a formal theory.

Figure 1.1: Timeline of key discoveries leading to the modern Cell Theory

Robert Hooke (1665)

Using a self-built compound microscope, Hooke examined a thin slice of cork and observed row upon row of small, empty box-like compartments. He named them "cellulae" (Latin for small rooms). What he actually saw were the dead cell walls of cork tissue — not living cells — since cork is non-living plant tissue.

Anton von Leeuwenhoek (1674)

Using hand-ground lenses of very high quality, Leeuwenhoek was the first person to see and describe a living cell. He observed free-living cells in pond water — bacteria, protozoa (which he called "animalcules"), and even blood cells and sperm cells.

Robert Brown (1831)

A Scottish botanist who, while studying orchid cells under the microscope, discovered a dense, roughly spherical structure inside every cell and named it the nucleus. This was the first organelle to be described.

Purkinje (1839)

Introduced the term "protoplasm" for the fluid substance of the cell, later understood to be the living material — the physical basis of life — within which the nucleus and organelles are suspended.

The Cell Theory — Formal Statement
  • M. Schleiden (1838): German botanist; examined many plant tissues and concluded that all plants are composed of cells.
  • T. Schwann (1839): British zoologist; extended the idea to animal tissues, discovered the plasma membrane, and noted that the cell wall is unique to plant cells. Together, Schleiden and Schwann formulated the Cell Theory: all living organisms are composed of cells and cells are the basic unit of life.
  • R. Virchow (1855): Modified the theory by adding its most important corollary — "Omnis cellula-e cellula" (all cells arise from pre-existing cells) — explaining that cells are not spontaneously generated but divide to produce new cells.
Exceptions Worth Remembering
  • Viruses are acellular and non-living outside a host — they do not obey the Cell Theory since they lack their own cellular machinery.
  • Mycoplasma are the smallest known cells (about 0.3 μm), small enough to pass through bacteria-proof filters, and lack a definite cell wall.

All living organisms fall into two broad categories based on cellular organisation: prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Prokaryotic cells are the simplest and most ancient form of cellular life — they include Bacteria, Blue-Green Algae (Cyanobacteria), Mycoplasma, and PPLO (Pleuro Pneumonia Like Organisms). They are generally smaller (1–10 μm) and structurally simpler than eukaryotic cells, and they lack a membrane-bound nucleus — their genetic material lies directly in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid — as well as other membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria, ER, and Golgi bodies.

Figure 1.2: Generalised structure of a prokaryotic cell (bacterium)

Cell Envelope

  • Glycocalyx: Outermost, sugary coat. A loose, undefined sheath = Slime Layer; a thick, tough, well-organised layer = Capsule. Protects against desiccation and host immune attack.
  • Cell Wall: Made of peptidoglycan (murein) — chains of sugars cross-linked by short peptides. Gives shape to the cell and prevents it from bursting or collapsing due to osmotic pressure changes.
  • Plasma Membrane: Selectively permeable structure lying just beneath the wall; controls entry and exit of substances.

Specialized Structures

  • Mesosomes: Vesicular/tubular infoldings of the plasma membrane that increase surface area; help in cell wall formation, DNA replication and its distribution to daughter cells, and in respiration.
  • Ribosomes: 70S type (made of 50S + 30S subunits), found freely in the cytoplasm. When several ribosomes attach to a single strand of mRNA, the chain is called a Polysome/Polyribosome, translating the message simultaneously.
  • Inclusion Bodies: Reserve, non-membrane-bound material — phosphate granules, cyanophycean granules, glycogen granules, and gas vacuoles (in aquatic/blue-green algae, for buoyancy).
  • Motility: The flagellum (filament + hook + basal body) drives movement. Pili and fimbriae are hair-like appendages used only for attachment to surfaces or other cells, never for movement.
Common Bacterial Shapes

Coccus (spherical), Bacillus (rod-shaped), Vibrio (comma-shaped), and Spirillum (spiral) — the four basic shapes bacteria are classified into.

Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer & Nicolson, 1972)

The plasma membrane is not a rigid boundary — it behaves like a two-dimensional fluid, with lipid and protein molecules able to move sideways within the layer, much like icebergs floating and drifting in a sea of lipids. This is why the model is called "fluid" (molecules move) and "mosaic" (proteins are studded throughout, like tiles in a mosaic).

Figure 1.3: Fluid Mosaic Model — a phospholipid bilayer studded with integral and peripheral proteins
  • The membrane is built from a phospholipid bilayer: hydrophilic (water-loving) heads face outward toward water on both sides, while hydrophobic (water-fearing) tails face inward, shielded from water.
  • In the human RBC membrane, proteins make up ~52% and lipids ~40% of the membrane mass — proteins are the functional workhorses (transporters, receptors, enzymes).
  • Integral Proteins: Partly or wholly buried within/spanning the membrane; many act as channels or carriers.
  • Peripheral Proteins: Loosely attached to the inner or outer surface; do not span the bilayer.
  • Transport across the membrane: Passive transport (diffusion/osmosis) moves substances down their concentration gradient with no energy cost, while Active transport moves substances against the gradient and requires ATP — e.g. the Na⁺/K⁺ pump, which pumps 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ pumped in.

Cell Wall & Connections

Unlike the membrane, the cell wall is a rigid, non-living structure that lies outside the plasma membrane in plant cells, fungi, and algae — it gives mechanical support, shape, and protection, and prevents the cell from bursting when water enters by osmosis.

Figure 1.4: Middle lamella cementing two adjacent plant cell walls, connected by plasmodesmata
  • Algae: Wall made of cellulose, galactans, mannans, and often encrusted with calcium carbonate (CaCO₃).
  • Plants: Wall made of cellulose, hemicellulose, pectins, and structural proteins.
  • Middle Lamella: A shared layer made mainly of calcium and magnesium pectate that cements the walls of neighboring cells together.
  • Plasmodesmata: Cytoplasmic channels/bridges that pass through the wall, connecting the cytoplasm of adjacent plant cells and allowing molecules to move directly between them.

The endomembrane system refers to a group of membranous organelles that work together as a coordinated production line — manufacturing, packaging, modifying, and shipping proteins and lipids to their correct destination inside or outside the cell. It includes the Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER), Golgi Apparatus, Lysosomes, and Vacuoles. (Note: mitochondria, chloroplasts, and peroxisomes are not considered part of this system since they don't originate from the ER-Golgi pathway.)

Figure 1.5: The secretory pathway — proteins move from RER → Golgi → vesicles → their final destination

Endoplasmic Reticulum

  • RER (Rough ER): Studded with ribosomes on its outer surface; the main site of protein synthesis and secretion — proteins are threaded into the ER lumen as they are made.
  • SER (Smooth ER): Devoid of ribosomes; the site of lipid and steroid hormone synthesis, and in liver cells, also involved in detoxification of drugs and poisons.

Golgi Apparatus

Discovered by Camillo Golgi, 1898

  • Made of flat, membrane-bound sacs called cisternae, stacked like a pile of plates, with a Cis face (forming/convex, facing the ER) that receives material and a Trans face (maturing/concave) that ships it out.
  • Acts as the cell's "packaging and dispatch office" — the primary site for synthesis of glycoproteins and glycolipids, and for sorting proteins to their correct address.

Lysosomes & Vacuoles

  • Lysosomes: Membrane-bound "suicide bags" rich in hydrolytic enzymes, active at acidic pH (~5.0); digest worn-out organelles, foreign material, and can dismantle their own cell (autolysis).
  • Vacuoles: Fluid-filled sacs bound by a single membrane called the Tonoplast, which pumps ions/solutes against their gradient. In mature plant cells, the central vacuole can occupy up to 90% of cell volume, maintaining turgor pressure.

Mitochondria and plastids are unique among organelles: each carries its own small circular double-stranded DNA, its own 70S ribosomes, and can divide independently of the cell, on its own schedule. This is why they are called semi-autonomous organelles — "semi" because although they can grow and divide by themselves, they still depend on the nucleus for many of the proteins they need. The Endosymbiotic Theory explains this: both organelles are thought to have originated from free-living prokaryotes that were engulfed by an ancestral eukaryotic cell and became permanent residents.

Figure 1.6: Mitochondrion — cristae increase the surface area for respiration

Mitochondria

  • The "Powerhouse of the Cell" — site of aerobic respiration and ATP generation.
  • Double membrane-bound: a smooth outer membrane and a highly folded inner membrane forming finger-like infoldings called cristae, which increase surface area for the enzymes of respiration.
  • The fluid interior enclosed by the inner membrane is the matrix, containing mitochondrial DNA, ribosomes, and enzymes.
Figure 1.7: Chloroplast — grana stacks embedded in the stroma

Plastids

  • Chloroplasts: Site of photosynthesis; contain a fluid stroma and stacks of membranous sacs called thylakoids (a stack = granum), where chlorophyll captures light energy.
  • Chromoplasts: Contain carotenoid pigments, giving fruits and flowers red, orange, and yellow colours.
  • Leucoplasts: Colourless, storage plastids —
    • Amyloplasts: Store carbohydrates/starch.
    • Elaioplasts: Store oils and fats.
    • Aleuroplasts: Store proteins.

Ribosomes & Cytoskeleton

  • Eukaryotic Ribosomes: Larger 80S type (made of 60S + 40S subunits), first described by George Palade and hence also called Palade granules. Found free in the cytoplasm or attached to the RER.
  • Cytoskeleton: A dynamic internal scaffolding network of microtubules (thickest, made of tubulin), microfilaments (thinnest, made of actin), and intermediate filaments — together they give the cell its shape, mechanical strength, and enable organelle movement and cell motility.

Cilia, Flagella & Centrosomes

Cilia and flagella are hair-like projections from the cell surface built around a core of microtubules called the axoneme, covered by the plasma membrane. Cilia are short and numerous, beating in coordinated waves (e.g. lining the respiratory tract); flagella are longer and fewer, driving cells forward (e.g. sperm).

Figure 1.8: Axoneme (9+2) of cilia/flagella vs. Centriole (9+0) cross-sections
  • Cilia / Flagella Axoneme: 9 + 2 arrangement — 9 peripheral doublets of microtubules surrounding 2 central singlets, generating the beating/whip-like motion.
  • Centrosome & Centrioles: 9 + 0 arrangement — 9 peripheral triplets arranged around a central hub with spokes (like a cartwheel), with no central microtubule pair. The centrosome usually contains two centrioles lying at right angles (90°) to each other.
  • Function: Centrioles form the basal bodies from which cilia/flagella grow, and organize the spindle fibers that segregate chromosomes during cell division.

Nucleus Structure

The nucleus is the "control center" of the eukaryotic cell, discovered by Robert Brown, and houses nearly all the cell's genetic material.

Figure 1.9: Nucleus — double envelope with pores, nucleolus, and chromatin
  • Bound by a double envelope (two membranes), perforated by nuclear pores that regulate the two-way traffic of molecules (like RNA and proteins) between the nucleus and cytoplasm.
  • Nucleolus: A dense, non-membrane-bound body inside the nucleus; the active site for rRNA synthesis and ribosome subunit assembly.
  • Chromatin: The thread-like complex of DNA and histone proteins that fills the nucleus (term coined by W. Flemming); it condenses into visible chromosomes only when the cell is about to divide.

Chromosome Types (by centromere position)

Figure 1.10: Chromosome shapes based on where the centromere (red) sits along the chromosome (green)
  • Metacentric: Centromere exactly in the centre — produces two equal arms, giving a "V" shape at anaphase.
  • Sub-metacentric: Centromere slightly off-centre — one arm a little longer, giving an "L" shape.
  • Acrocentric: Centromere near one end — one very short arm and one long arm, giving a "J" shape.
  • Telocentric: Centromere right at the terminal tip — a single arm, giving an "I" shape.
  • Satellite Chromosome: A chromosome bearing a small segment attached beyond a non-staining secondary constriction.

Microbodies

Microbodies are small, single membrane-bound sacs found scattered in the cytoplasm of both plant and animal cells, containing various enzymes. Key examples include peroxisomes, which break down fatty acids and detoxify harmful substances by generating and then degrading hydrogen peroxide, and glyoxysomes (found in germinating fatty seeds), which convert stored fats into carbohydrates.

Chapter 2: Overview of the Cell Cycle

The Cell Cycle is the precise sequence of events by which a cell duplicates its genome, synthesizes its organelles, and eventually divides into two daughter cells.

  • Cytoplasmic Growth: A continuous process throughout the cycle.
  • DNA Replication: Occurs strictly during one specific stage (S phase).
Duration Variations:
  • Human Cell in culture: ~24 hours
  • Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae): ~90 minutes
  • E. coli: ~20 minutes
Figure 1.1: Schematic representation of the Cell Cycle ring & phases

Detailed Breakdown of Interphase

Interphase is the biosynthetic preparatory phase where the cell prepares for division through ordered cell growth and DNA replication.

G1 Phase (Gap 1)

Post-mitotic phase. The cell is metabolically active and continuously grows without replicating DNA.

Genetics: 2n Chromosomes | 2C DNA

S Phase (Synthesis)

DNA replication takes place inside the nucleus. Centrioles duplicate in cytoplasm of animal cells.

Genetics: 2n Chromosomes | 4C DNA (Doubled!)

G2 Phase (Gap 2)

Synthesis of tubulin proteins required for spindle fibers. Mitochondria & chloroplasts duplicate.

Genetics: 2n Chromosomes | 4C DNA
G0 Phase (Quiescent Stage): Cells that do not divide further (e.g., mature neurons, heart cells) exit the G1 phase to enter G0. They remain metabolically active but no longer proliferate unless stimulated.
Figure 2.1: DNA content (C-value) rises from 2C to 4C across G1 → S → G2

Mitosis (Equational Division)

Mitosis occurs in somatic cells (2n → 2n), ensuring exact genetic identicalness in daughter cells.

Prophase (Longest Phase)

Figure 3.1: Prophase — chromatin condensing, centrosomes migrating to poles
  • Chromatin fibers condense into distinct compact chromosomes.
  • Centrosomes start moving toward opposite poles.
  • Nuclear envelope, nucleolus, Golgi body, and ER completely break down by late prophase.

Metaphase (Morphology Phase)

Figure 3.2: Metaphase — spindle fibers attach to kinetochores; chromosomes align centrally
  • Disintegration of nuclear envelope is complete.
  • Chromosomes line up along the equatorial Metaphase Plate.
  • Spindle fibers attach to disc-shaped kinetochores on centromeres.
  • Best stage to study chromosome morphology & count!

Anaphase (Shortest Phase)

Figure 3.3: Anaphase — centromeres split; daughter chromosomes move poleward
  • Centromere splits simultaneously; sister chromatids separate into individual daughter chromosomes.
  • Chromosomes move to opposite poles (V, L, J, I shapes visible based on centromere position).

Telophase (Reversal of Prophase)

Figure 3.4: Telophase — two new nuclei form at opposite poles
  • Chromosomes reach poles and decondense into loose chromatin.
  • Nuclear envelope reforms around each chromosome cluster.
  • Nucleolus, Golgi apparatus, and ER reappear.

Meiosis (Reductional Division)

Meiosis occurs in germ cells (2n → n), producing four genetically distinct haploid daughter cells across two successive divisions.

Meiosis I — Reductional

Homologous chromosomes pair and separate; chromosome number is halved (2n → n).

Figure 4.2: Meiosis I — pairing/crossing over, then homolog separation
  • Prophase I subdivides into five sub-stages: Leptotene, Zygotene, Pachytene, Diplotene, Diakinesis.
  • Crossing over occurs during Pachytene, generating genetic recombination.
  • Bivalents/tetrads line up at Metaphase I; homologs (not sister chromatids) separate at Anaphase I.

Meiosis II — Equational

Resembles a normal mitotic division; sister chromatids finally separate.

Figure 4.3: Meiosis II — mitosis-like separation of sister chromatids
  • No further DNA replication occurs before Meiosis II.
  • Proceeds through its own Prophase II, Metaphase II, Anaphase II, and Telophase II.
  • Ends with four haploid, genetically unique cells.
Figure 4.1: Crossing over in Prophase I, homolog separation in Anaphase I, chromatid separation in Anaphase II
Prophase I Sub-stages (mnemonic: "Lazy Zebras Pack Dumb Dinner"):
  1. Leptotene: Chromosomes begin condensing, appear as long thin threads.
  2. Zygotene: Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) forming bivalents.
  3. Pachytene: Crossing over between non-sister chromatids occurs.
  4. Diplotene: Synaptonemal complex dissolves; chiasmata become visible.
  5. Diakinesis: Terminalization of chiasmata; chromosomes fully condense.
